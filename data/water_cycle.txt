The Water Cycle

The water cycle, also known as the hydrological cycle, describes the continuous movement of water on, above, and below the surface of the Earth. Water is constantly changing states between liquid, solid (ice), and gas (water vapor).

The cycle has four main stages: evaporation, condensation, precipitation, and collection.

Evaporation occurs when the Sun heats water in rivers, lakes, and oceans, turning it into water vapor that rises into the atmosphere. Plants also release water vapor through a process called transpiration. Together, evaporation and transpiration are called evapotranspiration.

Condensation happens when water vapor in the atmosphere cools and changes back into liquid water, forming clouds. Clouds are made of tiny water droplets or ice crystals suspended in the air.

Precipitation occurs when water droplets in clouds combine and grow heavy enough to fall to Earth as rain, snow, sleet, or hail. The type of precipitation depends on the temperature of the air.

Collection is the stage where water gathers in oceans, rivers, lakes, and groundwater. Some water soaks into the ground and becomes groundwater, which can remain underground for years before resurfacing in springs or being pumped from wells.

The water cycle is powered by solar energy and gravity. The Sun provides the energy for evaporation, and gravity pulls precipitation back to Earth and drives the flow of rivers toward the ocean.

Human activities affect the water cycle. Deforestation reduces transpiration, urban development increases runoff and reduces groundwater recharge, and climate change alters precipitation patterns and increases evaporation rates.

The total amount of water on Earth remains roughly constant — about 1.386 billion cubic kilometers. However, only about 2.5% is freshwater, and less than 1% of that is easily accessible for human use.

Understanding the water cycle is essential for managing water resources, predicting weather, and addressing environmental challenges such as droughts and floods.
